From LAER to DARE, and what it means outside software

2 November 2026 · 4 min read

In short

TSIA argues AI breaks the old Land, Adopt, Expand, Renew sequence and proposes Design, Activate, Realise, Evolve instead. The parts that travel to services are agreeing a measurable outcome before the contract, and measuring time to the first outcome rather than activity.

  • Customer Lifecycle
  • Value Realisation
  • Professional Services

Source

TSIA. Customer Lifecycle Management: What AI Changes, 21 October 2026.

Why I picked this up

LAER (Land, Adopt, Expand, Renew) has shaped customer success thinking for over a decade, so a proposal to replace it got my attention. I also wanted to test a hunch: that a framework built for AI software would have more to say to a services business than its title suggests.

What it says, in brief

TSIA’s article, Customer Lifecycle Management: What AI Changes, explains why AI strains the familiar lifecycle. Buying an AI solution and getting value from it are now very different things, because the solution has to work inside the customer’s data and systems before anything useful happens.

The article calls the distance between what’s sold and what delivery can demonstrate the Promise Gap, a term from TSIA’s J.B. Wood. Its answer is a new model, DARE: design the outcome before the sale, activate it with hands-on technical help, realise value by tying renewal and expansion to proven results, and keep evolving the solution as data and needs change. It recommends testing this on a single product line rather than redesigning everything at once.

What stayed with me

The Promise Gap. This is the idea I keep returning to. It names something every customer-facing team feels but rarely measures: the distance between what was sold and what anyone can show was delivered. Once it has a name, it’s hard to read a renewal conversation any other way.

Outcome before signature. The article suggests tracking the share of new deals that have an agreed, measurable outcome before the contract is signed. It’s a sharp measure. It moves the hardest conversation to the point where both sides have the most goodwill and the most room to change course.

Time to first measurable outcome. Replacing logins and feature usage with the time from signature to the first demonstrated result is the change I’d make first. Logins tell you someone turned up. They don’t tell you anything happened.

In professional services

Most services relationships have no product to log into, which makes this measure fit better, not worse. In a compliance professional services business serving schools and trusts, the first measurable outcome might be the first audit or review delivered, with its findings agreed and its actions owned. That’s a far better early signal than a portal login.

Agreeing the outcome before signature translates directly. Record what the client expects at the handoff from sales, and carry it into onboarding so delivery starts from the same page. It’s a small habit, and it removes a familiar problem: delivery discovering what was promised by accident.

Forward-deployed engineering doesn’t map onto services as it stands. Nobody is embedding engineers in a primary school. The principle does map: get someone close to the client early enough to clear the obstacles to the first result, whether that’s a missing contact, an unclear scope or a visit that keeps slipping.

Evolve is where services has the strongest story. In compliance, evolve looks like regulatory change and organisational change: new guidance, a school joining a trust, a new headteacher. It’s also the best answer to a client who feels they can now manage on their own. The ground keeps moving, so the value conversation should keep moving with it.

In a tech role

I’d follow the article’s advice and start with one product line. Redesigning the whole lifecycle at once is how frameworks turn into slide decks. Pick one line, agree an outcome measure that sales, services and delivery all sign up to, and connect one renewal motion to delivered value. If it works, there’s evidence to scale. If it doesn’t, finding out was cheap.

I’d also put time to first measurable outcome on the customer success dashboard from day one. It links implementation, CS and value roles, and it gives everyone the same definition of a good start. My AI agent go-live kit hands time to first value straight to the health score for exactly that reason: the go-live team and the CS team should be measuring the same thing.

The bigger shift in the article is cultural. Treating renewal as a checkpoint in continuous value realisation, rather than a negotiation at the end, changes who feels responsible for it. In a SaaS company, that responsibility should sit with whoever can see the outcomes, not only with whoever holds the renewal quota.

What I’m still thinking about

  • In services, who owns the outcome agreed at handoff once the original salesperson has moved on?
  • How do you agree a measurable outcome with a client whose main goal is that nothing goes wrong?
  • Is Evolve a new stage, or what good customer success has always looked like, with a better name?

That last question is half joke, half compliment. Either way, it’s the stage I’d most like to get right.
