Hours, pricing and what AI does to services work

15 November 2026 · 3 min read

In short

AI takes hours out of services work, and pricing decides who keeps the saving. In compliance services, where the client owns the outcome, productised and clearly scoped offers make more sense than pure outcome pricing.

  • Professional Services
  • Pricing
  • AI

Source

TSIA. AI in Professional Services: Will It Shrink PS?, 21 October 2026.

Why I picked this up

I'm not a pricing specialist, so I came to this as an outsider with one practical question. If AI makes services work faster, who keeps the time it saves?

What it says, in brief

TSIA's article, AI in Professional Services: Will It Shrink PS?, looks at professional services teams inside technology companies: the people who implement, integrate and optimise what the company sells. It argues that AI will take hours out of most projects, from discovery through to testing and documentation, leaving people to focus on scoping, design and stakeholder work.

It also points to new demand, from readiness work before deployment to optimisation after go-live. Its central claim is that pricing, more than AI itself, decides whether a services organisation shrinks or grows.

What stayed with me

Pricing decides who keeps the saving. This is the clearest idea in the piece. If work is sold by the hour, every hour AI saves is revenue lost. With a fixed fee, the provider keeps the saving until the next negotiation, when a well-informed buyer pushes the price down. With pricing aligned to outcomes, the saving becomes margin. Laid out like that, it's hard to unsee.

New demand looks like customer success. The readiness, activation and optimisation work the article describes is, in large part, customer success with a services invoice attached. That's encouraging for anyone who works across both, as I do.

Who trains the seniors? Much of the junior work in a project, such as documentation, configuration, testing and first drafts, is exactly what AI speeds up. The article suggests this points towards smaller teams weighted to senior people. It raises a real question about how future senior people learn, and I don't have an answer. Nobody gets good at scoping without first doing a lot of the work that's now being automated.

In professional services

In a compliance professional services business serving schools and trusts, pure outcome pricing is risky, because the client owns compliance. A provider can't sell compliance itself as an outcome when the decisions and the accountability sit with the school. Any priced outcome would need careful definition: reviews completed, actions closed, response times met, rather than a guarantee the provider can't control.

Productised, clearly scoped offers are the more relevant move. The article makes the point that repeatable packages give AI a process to speed up, and let a provider price the result rather than the hours. For a business supporting thousands of clients, a clear package is also simply easier to explain, sell and deliver consistently.

The new demand translates as well. Helping a school get ready for an inspection, or keeping a trust's arrangements current as guidance changes, is already readiness and optimisation work. Naming it that way makes its value easier for a client to see, and easier to price.

In a tech role

Understanding how services are priced changes how I'd think about time to value and scope. If implementation is sold by the hour, a faster go-live is good for the customer and awkward for the services budget. If it's fixed or hybrid, speed and margin pull in the same direction. A customer success or value role sits right on that seam, so it's worth knowing which model you're working under before promising a customer a faster start.

The article notes that few organisations jump straight to outcome pricing. Most start with a hybrid: a guaranteed base, plus a share tied to results. That's the right order. You can't price an outcome you can't yet measure, which brings it back to instrumentation and agreed success criteria, the part of value work I'm most drawn to.

It also changes how I'd read a statement of work. Scope written as hours invites a conversation about effort. Scope written as outcomes invites a conversation about value, and that's the conversation customer success should be leading.

What I'm still thinking about

  • If AI removes the junior work, where do future senior consultants get their practice?
  • How do you price a service whose best result is that nothing happens?
  • Should a services team share AI savings with clients up front, before the next negotiation forces the issue?

The second question is the one I'll keep coming back to. In compliance, the quiet years are the successful ones, and they're the hardest to put a price on.

These are my personal reading notes and reflections, not the views of any employer.
