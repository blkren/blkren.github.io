Where I’d let an agent act, and where I wouldn’t (yet)

11 November 2026 · 3 min read

In short

I’d let an AI agent triage, route, summarise and prepare. I’d keep a person on disputes, contracts, upset clients and anything with legal weight. And I’d fix the knowledge base and complete a data protection assessment before the agent answers anything.

  • Agentic AI
  • Customer Service
  • Governance

Source

TSIA. Agentic AI in Customer Service: What It Can't Do Yet, 21 October 2026.

Why I picked this up

Most writing about AI agents sells the upside. I picked this one up because its title promised the opposite: an honest account of the limits. In a business that gives compliance advice, the limits are the part I need to understand first.

What it says, in brief

TSIA’s article, Agentic AI in Customer Service: What It Can’t Do Yet, sets out where AI agents in technical support stand today. With accurate knowledge and the right system access, agents handle high-volume, well-documented requests well, from start to finish. They still struggle with diagnosis across several systems, policy exceptions and disputes, and upset or strategic customers.

The article treats a good deployment as a change to people, process and technology, with human agents moving into oversight roles. It argues for measuring verified resolution rather than deflection, because a deflected case can come back.

What stayed with me

Answer, suggest, act. The article’s distinction is the clearest I’ve read: a chatbot answers, a copilot suggests and an agent acts. I’ll be borrowing it. It moves the conversation from what a tool is called to what it’s allowed to change.

Thin knowledge fails silently. The line that stopped me was "Agents fail quietly." When the knowledge behind an agent is thin or out of date, it still gives a confident answer, and the mistake surfaces later as a repeat contact. That’s the risk that matters most in my world.

Deflection is not resolution. A deflected case can return, or turn into churn months later. Verified resolution and repeat contacts tell you whether the agent actually helped. Deflection only tells you it was busy.

In professional services

In a compliance professional services business serving schools and trusts, I’d start agents in four places: triage, routing, case summaries and account preparation. They’re low risk, the time saving is immediate, and a person still makes every decision that matters. My pre-call brief generator is built on the same idea: the model drafts, code checks every number, and a person decides.

I’d keep a person on disputes, contract interpretation, upset clients and anything with commercial or legal weight. That isn’t caution for its own sake. In compliance advice, a confident wrong answer is a real risk to the client, and the consequences land on a team whose whole job is getting it right.

That makes the knowledge base the first investment, not the last. Before an agent answers a single question, the guidance it draws on has to be current, consistent and owned by a named person. A well-maintained knowledge base helps the human team too, so it’s never wasted effort.

Data protection comes before any of it. Any AI that processes client data needs a proper data protection assessment first. A business that advises others on data protection should model good practice itself, and its clients will rightly expect it to.

In a tech role

Before any pilot, I’d want three guardrail tiers agreed in writing: what the agent may do alone, what needs a person’s approval, and what it must never touch. The article recommends exactly this, and it’s a conversation for product, support and legal to have before go-live, not after the first incident. It’s the same principle as the test gate in my AI agent go-live kit: decide the rules before the pressure arrives.

I’d define the measures before the pilot starts, too: verified resolution, repeat contacts on AI-handled cases, and satisfaction on those cases. If a pilot can only report deflection, it can’t tell you whether it worked.

The article’s section on people is the part I’d share with any leadership team. Roles change before headcount does. Someone has to review the agent’s decisions, maintain the knowledge it relies on and own the cases it escalates. If nobody is assigned to oversight, nobody catches the mistakes.

What I’m still thinking about

  • Who owns the knowledge base in a small team, when everyone also has a day job?
  • At what point does summarising a case become deciding what it means?
  • How do you tell a client, plainly, which parts of their service an agent touches?

I’m comfortable with the bracketed yet in the title. It’s doing a lot of work.

These are my personal reading notes and reflections, not the views of any employer.
