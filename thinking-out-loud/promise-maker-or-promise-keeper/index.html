Promise maker or promise keeper?

28 October 2026 · 5 min read

In short

Most effort goes into the first 90 days, but whether a customer stays is decided in the 900 that follow. I'm testing two small ways to keep the agreed outcome visible: recording it at handoff, and talking about value before budget and break dates.

Why this one stayed with me

I didn't expect a keynote about value realisation to open with 1990s country music, but here we are. George Humphrey began his closing talk at TSW 2026 by asking the room who liked country, then built the whole session around a Trisha Yearwood song about promises that are hard to keep.

It's a good hook, and the question I ask most in my own work: once we've told a client what they'll get, how do we make sure they actually get it, and how would either of us know?

What it says, in brief

George Humphrey is Senior VP of Research at TSIA, and this was the closing keynote at TSIA's Technology & Services World 2026. His argument runs like this.

The technology industry has spent decades building everything around the first 90 days: implement the solution, switch it on, move on. But subscription revenue depends on the next 900 days, when the customer decides whether to stay. Hoping that goes well isn't a plan.

He suggested that the later stages of the customer lifecycle, realising value and then evolving it, are the least developed. AI makes this more pressing, because AI solutions can lose impact quietly after launch as data and requirements shift. He shared a survey figure in the keynote suggesting that only around 5% of leaders feel very confident they can prove value at renewal. Whatever the exact number, the direction is clear.

His answer is mostly organisational rather than technical: join up the people who build a solution with the people who run it, measure the outcome before you hand over, bring finance into the conversation early, and swap status updates for genuine value reviews. He closed by asking the room one question: "Are you a promise maker or are you a promise keeper?"

What stayed with me

The 90 days and the 900. It's a simple contrast that reframes where the effort goes. Most of the energy in a customer relationship tends to gather around the start, when everything is new and visible. The part that decides whether the relationship lasts is longer, quieter and much easier to neglect.

Value fades quietly. This was the idea I kept turning over afterwards. The gap after go-live isn't defined by a crisis. It's defined by silence. Nothing breaks, nobody complains, and the value just thins out until someone asks at renewal what they've been paying for. I found it both obvious and slightly uncomfortable, which is usually a sign something is worth paying attention to.

Making the promise a record. He offered six practical moves, running from fusing the build and run teams through to pricing both the run and the result. The one that felt most usable straight away was making the promise a record: write down the outcome you've committed to, and bring it back into every value review. It stops being something sales remembers and becomes something everyone can check.

In professional services

I work in a compliance professional services business serving schools and trusts, where a small team supports thousands of clients, and a lot of the keynote's machinery doesn't translate. Forward-deployed operations, pods and engineers splitting their time with delivery are built for software companies at scale. A services team doesn't need to copy them. The principles travel much better than the org charts.

Silence is the clearest example. In my world, it looks like a school that simply stops asking for advice. That can mean things are going well, but it can also mean we've stopped feeling useful to them, and those two look identical from the outside until it's too late to tell them apart.

So I've started two things in my own work. Both are work in progress:

  • capturing the outcome a client expects at the point of handoff, so it isn't lost between the sale and onboarding
  • planning value conversations ahead of budget and break dates, rather than waiting for a renewal that, on long contracts, may be years away

The idea of bringing finance into the handoff also translates better than I expected. In a school or trust, the person who holds the budget is often not the person who uses the service day to day. Involving them earlier should make the later value conversation easier, because they'll already know what was agreed and why.

In a tech role

The keynote helped me see that deployment roles and value roles sit at different points of the same lifecycle. Forward-deployed and implementation work leans towards designing and activating a solution. Value and success roles lean towards realising and evolving it. I'm drawn to the realise and evolve end. I'd rather be the person checking that the promise was kept than the one making it.

Gating the handoff on measurement is the move I'd want to bring with me. Before anything goes live, agree how the outcome will be measured, and make sure the signals actually exist. It's close to the test gate idea in my AI agent go-live case study, just applied to value rather than safety.

The value review should replace the quarterly review outright, not sit alongside it. A review built around the outcome that was agreed, rather than around usage charts, gives both sides something real to talk about, and it's the meeting a finance lead will actually want to attend. Pricing the run and the result is the move I want to understand better, because it decides who benefits when the work gets more efficient.

What I'm still thinking about

  • How do you notice quiet early, without making every client feel watched?
  • If the promise becomes a record, who keeps it up to date when the client's goals change?
  • Can a small team run genuine value reviews across a large book of clients, or do most clients need a lighter version?

These are the questions I'm taking into the next budget season. In the meantime, I know which side of the promise I want to be on.
