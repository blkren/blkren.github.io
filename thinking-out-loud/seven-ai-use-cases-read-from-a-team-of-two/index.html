Seven AI use cases, read from a team of two

6 November 2026 · 4 min read

In short

Every one of TSIA's seven AI use cases depends on someone owning what happens when a signal fires. In a services business, where signals are structural rather than usage-based, I'd start with simple flags, plays and value reporting before anything predictive.

  • AI
  • Customer Success
  • Retention

Source

TSIA. AI for Customer Success: 7 Use Cases for Retention, 21 October 2026.

Why I picked this up

Most writing about AI in customer success assumes a software company with rich product data and a CS team large enough to have its own operations function. I wanted to read one of these lists from the other end: a small team supporting thousands of clients, where most of the useful signals don't come from logins at all. My first question was simple. Which of these would survive contact with my week?

What it says, in brief

TSIA's article, AI for Customer Success: 7 Use Cases for Retention, walks through seven ways AI can support retention, from churn prediction and health scoring through to value reporting and renewal forecasting. Its central point is that AI pays off when it's tied to one specific job with clean data behind it. It also argues that every use case should be judged by its effect on revenue retention, not by how many alerts it fires or emails it sends.

What stayed with me

Signals need owners. TSIA puts it in three words: "signals need owners". A churn score with no play behind it is just a dashboard that makes everyone feel worse. The article describes a loop where every signal reaches a named person, triggers an agreed response and gets its result tracked. Without the person and the response, a model only tells you what you're about to lose.

Value reporting the sponsor can use. Of the seven, this is the one I'd prioritise. The person who bought the service has to defend it to someone who controls a budget. Giving them something they can take straight to their finance lead does more for a renewal than any health score, because it equips the person who actually has to make the case.

Coverage beats accounts per CSM. The article suggests measuring how many accounts each CSM can cover while retention holds. At scale, that makes sense. At small scale, it's the wrong question. When a small team supports thousands of clients, what matters is whether the whole book is covered by something, whether that's a person, a programme or a well-timed email.

In professional services

In a compliance professional services business serving schools and trusts, the signals are structural rather than usage-based: a school joining a trust, a key contact leaving, a budget cycle, a break clause approaching. None of these needs a model to spot. They need a calendar, a clean record and an agreed play.

So I'd start with simple flags and plays before anything predictive. A model can only learn from a clean history, and most services businesses don't have one yet. Predictive scoring earns its place later, once the basics have run long enough to produce data worth learning from.

Value reporting needs careful framing here. In compliance services the client stays responsible for their own compliance, so a report can never tell a school it is compliant. It can show risk reduced and assurance given: actions closed, reviews completed, issues caught early. That's a value story a school business manager can take to governors or a trust's finance lead.

Digital customer success is the other use case that translates directly. The article argues that digital programmes should run across the whole book, not just the long tail. In services that's even more true. When a small team supports thousands of clients, every client needs a baseline of contact that doesn't depend on someone remembering to call.

In a tech role

Two of the seven feel immediately practical in a SaaS or AI company.

The first is health scores that explain themselves. A score that says why it moved gives a CSM something to act on and a reason to trust it. A number that simply drops gives them neither. I built my AI agent health score around the same principle, with red-flag caps and a data confidence figure, so the score is honest about what it doesn't know.

The second is routing expansion signals to whoever owns expansion. The article is clear that a signal only creates revenue if it reaches the right person with its context attached. That's an operations problem as much as an AI one: name the owner, agree the handoff, and make sure the conversation starts from what the customer has already achieved.

I'd also hold the article's line on measurement. Judge each use case by retention and time to value. Activity is easy to count and tells you very little.

What I'm still thinking about

  • If the most useful signals are structural, how much of AI in customer success is really good data hygiene with better timing?
  • What does a value report look like when the outcome is a risk that didn't happen?
  • At what size does a predictive model start to earn its keep over a well-run set of flags?

I suspect the answer to the last one is later than most vendors would like, but that's a hunch, not a finding.

These are my personal reading notes and reflections, not the views of any employer.
