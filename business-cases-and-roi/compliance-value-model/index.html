Putting a number on compliance risk

A risk-based renewal model for a customer who wants to cancel. I built it to show how I turn an audit history into a business case a finance lead can sign off.

The organisation, actions and figures here are fictional. The approach is based on renewal conversations I've had in compliance services, where I've kept clients by setting the cost of non-compliance against the cost of support.

The situation

Northfield Learning Trust runs six schools. A new business manager is reviewing every budget line and plans to cancel the trust's fire safety, health and safety, and data protection support, worth £11,000 a year. On paper it looks like an easy saving.

The audit history tells a different story. Staff changes meant ownership of several actions was never handed over, and some high-risk items have been open for months.

How I built the case

  1. Discovery. Start with the customer's own data: the audit history, which actions are open, how long, and who owns them.
  2. Baseline. Put an expected cost on each open action: the probability it leads to enforcement within a year, multiplied by the likely impact (fine, legal costs and remediation). Fire door breaches matter here, because fire safety offences can attract unlimited fines.
  3. Value drivers. Risk removed by closing actions with specialist support, and the business manager's time saved by not chasing them alone.
  4. Options and total cost. Compare three options over three years: keep the support, bring compliance in-house, or cancel with no replacement.
  5. Scenarios. Run conservative, expected and high cases, and show every assumption so the customer can challenge it.

What the model shows

In the expected scenario, the trust is carrying about £20,900 a year of risk exposure from its open actions, almost twice what the support costs. Keeping the service is the cheapest option over three years once risk and time are counted.

Why I show the conservative case

In the conservative scenario, the financial return on its own is negative. I'd still show it. A value case that only works with optimistic numbers won't survive a finance review, and being open about it builds trust. It also moves the conversation to what the spreadsheet can't price: prosecution risk, the safety of pupils and staff, and the trust's reputation with its board and parents.

What happens after the renewal

The same model becomes the scorecard. I'd agree targets with the customer, such as every high-risk action owned within 30 days and closed within 90. Then I'd track progress in a shared dashboard and review it each term. At renewal, the conversation is about results already delivered, not a promise.
